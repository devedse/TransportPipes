# 5 · Lessons from other games, and what players actually feel

Role: player psychologist and game historian. The question: what has worked and failed when people play together
(especially in idle games, logistics games and persistent worlds), why, and what TransportPipes should take.

---

## A. What players actually feel (the short model)

1. **Relationships need a choice.** A partner you picked (or a friend you brought) feels like a relationship. A
   neighbour you were assigned, who trades with you automatically, feels like infrastructure, like the water company.
   This is the root of the "district mega-network" failure: nobody chose anybody, so nobody cared about anybody.
   Consent plus visibility ("we shook hands, here is our pipe, here is what flows") turns trade into a relationship.
2. **Small groups carry the warmth, big groups carry the spectacle.** Dunbar's layers (about 5, 15, 50, 150) show up
   in every game: the 3–5 people you actually know (the crew), the 15–50 you recognise (a station, a clan, a Hay Day
   neighbourhood of 30), the server (Wonders, rankings). Each layer needs its own thing to do. Three friends live in
   the first layer; design for them first and the rest is the backdrop.
3. **Helping feels better than being helped**, as long as helping costs little and is thanked. Being helped too much
   feels like charity or debt (reciprocity pressure). The best social idle mechanics are cheap, asynchronous,
   one-directional favours that the receiver can't "owe" (Clash of Clans troop donations, Hay Day crate help,
   Forge of Empires motivate).
4. **Absence must not hurt anyone.** In async games the strongest negative feeling is not losing, it is *being the
   reason someone else lost* (guilt) or *being stalled by someone who isn't there* (resentment). Both kill groups.
   The fix is always the same: absent players degrade gracefully into something an NPC could be.
5. **People avoid empty restaurants.** Social proof decides where players settle and whether they stay. A world
   that *looks* busy (lit windows, moving goods, recent names) retains even solo players; a map of grey abandoned
   plots tells them the party is over (late Travian rounds, dead MMO servers).
6. **Comparison should be local.** Being 500th of 2,000 is depressing; being 3rd of 25 similar players is exciting
   ("big fish, little pond"). Veterans crushing a ranking is fine only if newcomers never have to look at it.
7. **Return shame is real.** Players who lapse avoid coming back if a mess, a scolding or a debt waits for them
   (Animal Crossing weeds and "you've been gone so long"). A warm, clean, rewarding return brings them back.
8. **Inequity aversion beats greed.** People refuse a deal that feels unfair even when it is good for them (the
   ultimatum game). A trade system where one side can be fleeced, or a goal where one player carries everybody,
   breeds resentment on *both* sides.

## B. Lessons, game by game

Format: **what it did → why it worked or failed → what TransportPipes should take.**

### Travian (and Tribal Wars, OGame)
- **Did:** you pick a quadrant at registration; alliances; beginner protection; a round ends with a Wonder of the
  World race; *account sitters* (a friend may run your account while you're away).
- **Why:** quadrant choice gave friends a rough way to start together, but only roughly; you could still land far
  apart. Beginner protection ends and veterans farm newcomers, and inactive villages become "farms": the world fills
  with grey ruins while the active players vanish into a few big alliances. Late in a round servers feel dead.
  Sitters were loved: they made a hardcore game survivable for people with lives.
- **Take:** let a friend's invite place you exactly, not roughly. Never let a lapsed player become loot or a grey
  ruin on the map. Steal the sitter idea in its harmless form: a trusted friend can keep your contracts and storage
  running, but can't spend your gold.

### Forge of Empires
- **Did:** neighbourhoods are regularly reshuffled; daily one-click "motivate/polish" on others' buildings; guild
  members invest Forge Points into each other's Great Buildings for rewards; plundering neighbours; guild
  expeditions and battlegrounds.
- **Why:** one-click aid is the single most-used social feature: cheap, async, visible, rewards both sides. The
  community spontaneously invented "1.9 threads" (structured investment swaps) out of the Great Building mechanic:
  players build cooperation when a mechanic rewards both sides. But reshuffled neighbourhoods mean you never bond with
  your neighbours, plunder makes newcomers feel like prey, and the guild activities became daily chores with
  attendance spreadsheets.
- **Take:** a daily one-tap favour on a friend's building (with a cap that can bank up for a few days). Let players
  invest in each other's projects with a guaranteed fair return. Keep neighbours stable, and never let them take
  anything from you.

### Clash of Clans
- **Did:** clans of 50; asymmetric troop donations (veterans fill newcomers' castles); clan wars, then later a
  **war preference opt-out**; Clan Games (a shared reward tier, each player's points capped); Clan Capital (a
  shared base built from everyone's donations, raided on weekends).
- **Why:** donations work because helping costs the veteran little and helps the newcomer a lot. Forced wars made
  clans kick inactive or casual members and made people feel guilty for not attacking, which is why the opt-out
  came. Per-player caps in Clan Games stop one player carrying the clan and let everyone matter. Clan Capital got a
  mixed reception: one more weekly chore.
- **Take:** asymmetric help (veterans feed newcomers' starts and get recognition for it). Every group activity is
  opt-in per player, and goals scale to the opted-in players. Cap individual contributions to shared goals.

### Hay Day (and Township)
- **Did:** roadside shop plus a **newspaper** that shows random players' shop ads; boat and truck orders where
  neighbours can **fill your crates** for you; neighbourhoods of 30; the weekly **Derby**.
- **Why:** the newspaper makes the world feel populated even for a solo player with no friends, and buying from
  strangers is effortless. Crate help is the perfect async favour: you ask, someone fills it hours later, both get
  rewarded, nobody needs to be online. The Derby is the famous trap: neighbourhoods demand you complete tasks,
  kick slackers and run leader drama; it turns a cosy game into a job.
- **Take:** a station **order board** where neighbours can fill each other's requests asynchronously, with an NPC
  that quietly fills what's left at the deadline so nobody is "let down". A newspaper-style feed of other players'
  surplus. No competition that requires everyone's attendance.

### SimCity BuildIt
- **Did:** Trade Depot and the Global Trade HQ (buy other players' listings); the Mayor's Contest in weekly leagues
  of similar players; Clubs with Club Wars (throw disasters at each other's cities).
- **Why:** the trade HQ lets solo players benefit from the crowd without any social cost. Leagues give everyone a
  winnable competition. Club Wars were complicated and destructive: damage to *your* city from people you don't
  know feels bad in a city builder, where people love what they built.
- **Take:** leagues, yes; destructive interaction in a builder, never. A cosy builder's competition must be
  "who builds/delivers more", never "who breaks whose".

### Settlers Online / Anno Online
- **Did:** buffing friends' buildings, co-op adventures, trade between players.
- **Why:** buffing is a gentle reason to visit someone else's island daily. But where the social layer is thin,
  players drift into pure solo play and the "online" part feels like overhead (Anno Online was shut down in 2018).
- **Take:** a reason to *visit* (a buff, a gift, a look at their works) beats a menu of trade numbers.

### OpenTTD multiplayer (and Transport Tycoon)
- **Did:** several companies on one map; friends can **join the same company** and build together; competing
  companies split the cargo of an industry by station rating; in vanilla you can't use another company's tracks.
- **Why:** joining one company is the simplest and best-loved co-op mode: no trade rules, just "our network". But
  competing companies on a shared map block each other with track, steal each other's cargo and grab the good
  spots; public servers need rules and admins.
- **Take:** a shared identity for the friends from minute one (a crew with one name, colour and network) is more
  fun than three companies that trade. Avoid any shared-space mechanic where an exclusive claim can block someone.

### Factorio and Satisfactory multiplayer
- **Did:** Factorio: one shared factory; players specialise on their own ("I'll do oil, you do trains"); shared
  conventions (the main bus, train standards) let people cooperate without talking; permission groups were added
  after public-server griefing. Satisfactory: for years the world only ran while the host was online.
- **Why:** specialisation emerges naturally when people share a space and goals, and nobody *has* to; standards
  make many hands work. Host-dependent worlds fail async play: the friend who plays at night can't play.
- **Take:** offer specialisation as a choice with a reward, not a requirement. Give a standard way to connect (a
  station manifold, a standard gate) so cooperation needs no negotiation. Nothing ever waits for someone to be online.

### Eve Online
- **Did:** corporations; null-sec territory and sovereignty wars; a safe high-sec where most players live; the NPC
  corporation as the default home of solo players; player corps for newbies (Eve University); courier contracts
  with collateral; whole player companies doing logistics (Red Frog Freight).
- **Why:** courier contracts with collateral let strangers trade safely, and logistics became a proud profession.
  But territory turned into a job ("spreadsheets in space"), the big blocs dominate, and newcomers mostly never leave
  safe space. The default NPC corp keeps solo players inside the social fabric without obligation.
- **Take:** logistics as a respected role (carrying for others with guarantees). A default "public" group for solo
  players. Territory must never be scarce or defensible-against-newcomers.

### Animal Crossing
- **Did:** visiting islands (Dodo codes), gifts, letters; turnip prices differ per island, so visiting the right
  friend on the right day is valuable (the community built queues like Turnip Exchange); long absences punished
  (weeds, cockroaches, villagers saying they missed you or moving away).
- **Why:** visiting matters when places *differ* (local prices). Gifts with no return obligation feel warm. The
  absence punishments created real return shame; many players say they avoided reopening the game for months.
- **Take:** local differences between stations (prices, deposits, demand) give a real reason to look at and pipe
  to other places. A return must be welcoming, never a clean-up job.

### Stardew Valley co-op
- **Did:** a shared farm; money shared at first, separate wallets added later; the day ends only when everyone
  goes to bed; the host must be online.
- **Why:** shared money caused friction (one friend spends, the other resents), hence the separate-wallets option.
  The sleep sync means the slowest player sets the pace for all.
- **Take:** friends share a place and a name, not a wallet. Never sync time between players.

### Egg, Inc. (the most relevant idle co-op)
- **Did:** co-op contracts: a small group joins with a code, shares one big goal and a deadline, each farm idles on
  its own. Then: "sleepers" (players who join and do nothing) and kicking drama; the community built a Discord
  (Majeggstics) to match active players; the game later added contract grades (matching by skill/activity).
- **Why:** shared goals in an idle game work beautifully async. The failure mode is matching active players with
  inactive ones, which makes the active feel exploited and the inactive feel guilty. Matching by activity fixed most
  of it.
- **Take:** when the game puts strangers together (stations, goals), match them by activity and stage. Reward each
  player for their own contribution so a sleeper costs nobody anything.

### Idle games with social layers (Tap Titans 2, AFK Arena, Cookie Clicker)
- **Did:** clan/guild raids where every member has a fixed number of attacks per day against a shared boss.
  Cookie Clicker stays essentially solo; its "social" life is sharing screenshots and saves.
- **Why:** fixed daily attempts equalise contribution between whales and casuals and are async. Solo idle games prove
  the core loop must be complete alone; the social layer is a bonus.
- **Take:** shared projects take a limited, banked number of "deliveries that count" per player.

### Minecraft servers (towns, Towny, Hypixel Skyblock)
- **Did:** a spawn town everyone passes through; player towns with claims; road and rail networks (nether highways)
  built as public works; plugins like Towny let inactive towns fall to ruins and claims expire; Skyblock co-op
  islands for friends.
- **Why:** public infrastructure built by players is what people remember and are proud of. Claims that never
  expire freeze the map; ruins are at least honest, but a map of ruins is depressing.
- **Take:** shared trunk pipes as public works with the builders' names on them. Land returns to the world after
  inactivity, without destroying the player's work (pack it, don't wreck it).

### Ultima Online (and FFXIV housing)
- **Did:** UO: a land rush, then all land taken; newcomers couldn't get a house; houses decayed when the owner was
  gone, and collapsing houses became looting spectacles. FFXIV: housing wards; a house is auto-demolished after about
  45 days of owner inactivity, with the furniture kept for the owner.
- **Why:** scarce land plus permanent claims locks newcomers out. FFXIV's rule frees land and still respects the
  owner, who can come back and rebuild.
- **Take:** land is never scarce (new stations open on demand), and an inactive estate is packed up, not destroyed.

### Foxhole
- **Did:** a persistent war where most of the game is logistics: trucking supplies to the front; regiments;
  public stockpiles; commendations.
- **Why:** logistics feels heroic when other players visibly depend on it and say thanks. Anyone joining mid-war
  has an obvious, useful job. The downsides: toxicity, griefing by stealing from public stockpiles, burnout.
- **Take:** make supply lines to a shared project *visible and thanked* (the pipe glows, the names are on it).
  Public stockpiles need withdraw limits.

### Death Stranding (and Journey, Dark Souls)
- **Did:** an asynchronous "strand" system: roads, bridges and ladders built by other players appear in your
  world; the only possible reaction is a "like"; no negative interaction exists. Journey: anonymous companions,
  communication only by chirps. Dark Souls: messages and ghosts of other players.
- **Why:** a lonely game felt connected without anyone being online at the same time and without any risk of
  griefing. Positive-only interaction makes it safe for shy players.
- **Take:** this is the template for the lonely player in TransportPipes: other players' pipes, pump stations and
  trunk lines appear and help you; you can only thank them.

### Dwarf Fortress / RimWorld (stories)
- **Did:** systems that generate stories; legendary community "succession games" (Boatmurdered) where a fortress
  passes from player to player each in-game year, with a written chronicle.
- **Why:** players remember stories, not numbers. A chronicle turns a log of events into shared memory, and the
  succession game shows async co-op can be about handing over, not playing at once.
- **Take:** an auto-written station chronicle; estates (or a project) can be handed over to a friend.

### Catan and Ticket to Ride
- **Did:** Catan: initial placement is the most strategic and talked-about moment; trading with players beats the
  bank's 4:1 / ports' 3:1, so the bank is the fallback; "leader bashing" (nobody trades with the leader).
  Ticket to Ride: claiming a route blocks it for others.
- **Why:** choosing a position with clear information is fun in itself. A bad-but-always-available fallback makes
  player trade attractive without making it necessary. Exclusive claims create blocking and hate.
- **Take:** make choosing a station a small, joyful decision with clear information (who, how active, what land).
  The hub stays the bank. No exclusive claims on shared routes.

### FarmVille (and the Facebook era)
- **Did:** expansions and buildings needed a number of "neighbours"; gift requests spammed friends.
- **Why:** gating progress on friend count produced spam, fake "add me" friends, and solo players stuck behind a
  wall. Short-term growth, long-term resentment.
- **Take:** never gate progress on the number of friends. Invitations should be a gift to the invitee, not a toll.

### Eco
- **Did:** a server economy where skills force specialisation, so you must trade to progress, within a deadline.
- **Why:** with enough active players it's wonderful; on a small or quiet server, forced dependency stalls
  everyone, and the game later added server settings to soften it.
- **Take:** dependency on other players must always have an NPC fallback.

### Guild Wars 2, Pokémon Go, Duolingo, Habitica
- **Guild Wars 2:** shared events where everyone gets the full reward, no competition for nodes, level downscaling
  so veterans can play with newcomers. **Take:** cooperation between unequal players must never feel like a loss
  for either.
- **Pokémon Go:** gyms held forever by veterans until the 2017 rework added decay and caps. **Take:** any "held"
  position needs decay or rotation.
- **Duolingo leagues:** weekly leagues of about 30 similar players, promotion and demotion. **Take:** the model for
  meaningful competition at any scale.
- **Habitica:** during boss quests, your missed tasks damage the whole party. **Take:** the textbook guilt trap;
  never let one player's absence damage the group.

## C. Ideas for TransportPipes (11)

Each idea names the lessons it comes from and the checklist items (1–14 from the brief) it serves.

### 1. Stations: a small place you choose, built around a shared pipe junction
A **station** is a cluster of 4–6 estates around a central **junction** (a manifold/plaza block). Every estate has
one gate onto the junction; goods traded inside a station travel over the junction, visibly, with no third party in
the way. A 24-estate district is the 15–50 layer and too big to know your neighbours; 4–6 is the right size for
"three friends plus one or two".
Between stations run longer trunk pipes (late game).
- *From:* OpenTTD stations, FFXIV housing wards, Hay Day neighbourhoods (but smaller), Dunbar.
- *Why it feels right:* a small, stable, named place with neighbours you recognise; the junction is a shared
  object that everyone's pipes plug into, so cooperation is literally visible on the map.
- *Checklist:* 1, 2, 9, 10, 11 (networks are bounded by the station; trunk lines are explicit links).

### 2. The invite link that holds a seat
"Join me at Copperford Station" creates an account and puts the friend in the next seat of *your* station. A
station founder can **reserve seats** for friends (held by an NPC tenant for up to 2–3 weeks; the tenant moves out
when the friend arrives, as tenants already do). A friend who joins a week later gets a **catch-up bonus** (XP
boost until they reach the station's median level, a starter crate from each friend).
- *From:* Travian quadrants (but exact), Clash clan links, recruit-a-friend programmes, Guild Wars downscaling.
- *Why:* the very first minute of the friends' story is "we live together". The latecomer is welcomed, not behind.
- *Checklist:* 1, 2, 5, 13 (a reason to invite: the inviter gets a cosmetic/banner piece, never a power boost).

### 3. The station board: choose a home with lit windows
A map/list of stations with open seats, each with: occupants (names, levels, banners), **activity as lit
windows** (played in the last day/week), what they sell and look for ("needs energy"), the land's deposits and
biome, and a local price quirk. A big **"Recommend me"** button picks a station with active players of a similar
stage and complementary needs (or a fresh station with friendly tenants). Moving is free in the first 3 days and
affordable later (see idea 7).
- *From:* Catan initial placement, Egg Inc. matching by activity, Hay Day newspaper, social proof.
- *Why:* choosing is a small pleasure when the information is good; the default protects those who don't care.
  Never show dead stations; they don't exist (idea 6).
- *Checklist:* 2, 3, 9.

### 4. A crew from minute one (lighter than a corporation)
Up to 4 players (typically one station) can form a **crew** at Level 1: a shared name, colour and banner on the
map, a crew chat, free trade between members (goods, not gold), a shared "crew network" colour for their pipes,
and small shared goals. Corporations (Level 8+) stay as the bigger layer. Separate wallets, always.
- *From:* OpenTTD joining a company, Stardew co-op (minus the shared wallet), Skyblock co-op islands.
- *Why:* identity is the cheapest, strongest social glue, and friends want it on day one, not at Level 8.
- *Checklist:* 1, 5, 9, 13.

### 5. Favours: one tap, async, always thanked
Daily, cheap, positive-only actions on others' estates: **tune-up** a friend's building (+10% for 8 h), **leave
a crate** of surplus at their gate, **fill an order** on the station board (idea 8). Favours bank up for up to 3
days so missing a day costs nothing. The receiver sees "Mira tuned up your Foundry" with a thank-you button; the
giver gets reputation ("Good Neighbour") and a small reward. Favours work for strangers in the station too, so solo
players receive and give them.
- *From:* FoE motivate, Settlers buffs, Clash donations, Animal Crossing gifts, Death Stranding likes.
- *Why:* helping feels good, costs little, needs nobody online; banking removes the daily-chore pressure.
- *Checklist:* 1, 3, 5, 7, 13.

### 6. Caretakers and packed estates (players who stop)
After 3 days away, a player's estate goes into **caretaker mode**: a caretaker tenant keeps running its trades at a
steady level, and any goods it fails to deliver to partners are filled by NPC imports at the usual price, so
partners never notice a break. After about 30–45 days (warned in advance), the estate is **packed into a deed**: a
blueprint of the whole estate (the blueprint feature exists) plus its storage; the land becomes a tenant plot or a
free seat. A returning player unpacks the deed into a free seat, ideally back in their station or next to their
crew, gets a "while you were away" story and a welcome-back crate. When a station empties, its remaining players
get a free offer to merge into a livelier station, so the map stays busy.
- *From:* FFXIV demolition-with-storage, Towny claim expiry, Travian sitters, Animal Crossing (as the warning).
- *Why:* nobody is stalled or let down by an absent player, no grey ruins, no return shame.
- *Checklist:* 4, 7, 11, 12 (existing estates migrate as deeds).

### 7. Moving house
An estate can move to another station: pack it as a deed, unpack it in the new seat (free in the first 3 days,
then a fee or a weekly free move). Used for: joining friends who started elsewhere, leaving a dead or unpleasant
station, merging stations, migrating the testers' worlds.
- *From:* Animal Crossing house relocation, Eve moving out of high-sec, Minecraft "move to a friend's town".
- *Why:* a choice you can undo is a choice you dare to make; it also solves every "wrong neighbour" problem.
- *Checklist:* 1, 2, 4, 8 (you can always leave a griefer), 12.

### 8. The station order board (async requests with a safety net)
Anyone can post a request ("200 bricks by tomorrow evening, paying 1.2× hub price"). Neighbours fill it from
storage or over the junction at any time; the filler gets the pay plus reputation. At the deadline, a tenant
fills the remainder at the hub price, so the requester is never stuck and nobody failed anybody.
- *From:* Hay Day crates, Egg Inc. co-ops, Eve courier contracts.
- *Why:* the most natural "help me" in an idle game, fully async, with no guilt and no stall.
- *Checklist:* 3, 5, 7, 8 (price bands stop funnelling), 10.

### 9. Station works and trunk lines (the shared late-game project)
A **station works** (water tower, rail depot, district furnace) sits on the junction and needs a *continuous
flow* of several inputs for a week; each member can pipe a different input (natural specialisation), each
player's counted contribution is capped per day and bankable, rewards are per player for their own
contribution plus a shared tier for the station. Later, stations build **trunk lines** to each other or to rare
deposits in the wilderness: long pipe projects with pump stations, whose segments carry the builders' names; once
built, others can use them (and thank the builders).
- *From:* Clash Clan Games caps, Tap Titans raids, Foxhole supply lines, Minecraft highways, Death Stranding roads.
- *Why:* veterans get bigger logistics to work towards together; contribution is fair; pipes stay the heart; a
  public trunk line gives solo players a world built by others.
- *Checklist:* 6, 7, 8, 10, 13.

### 10. Leagues and the Station Cup (friendly competition)
Weekly contests ranked inside **leagues of ~25 companies of a similar stage**, with promotion and demotion. A
**Station Cup** compares stations on deliveries per active member (best 3 count, so small stations and solo
players aren't punished). Any "held" title (top supplier of the district) decays and rotates.
- *From:* Duolingo leagues, SimCity Mayor's Contest, Pokémon Go gym decay, Catan's leader bashing (avoid it by
  having no direct contest).
- *Checklist:* 6, 8, 13.

### 11. The station chronicle and a living feed
An auto-written daily gazette per station and a server feed: "Copperford shipped its first steel; Mira's pipe to
Ashvale now carries a third of the district's glass; Tom is back after a month." Shareable postcards of an estate.
Ambient life on the map: goods visibly moving through junctions and trunk lines, lit windows, names on pipes.
- *From:* Dwarf Fortress/RimWorld stories, Hay Day newspaper, Death Stranding presence, Wordle-style sharing.
- *Why:* stories make recognition, recognition makes reasons to invite, and a busy feed fixes the empty-world feel.
- *Checklist:* 3, 9, 13.

(Optional 12.) **Specialisation bonus, never a requirement:** goods that come from a station mate by pipe count
as "fresh" (+5–10% output for the buyer, a small premium for the seller), while the hub/tenant remains the
always-there bank at a worse price. Friends make you better; nobody makes you stuck. *From:* Catan bank, Eco's
lesson, Factorio specialisation. *Checklist:* 3, 5, 10.

## D. The psychological traps and how to avoid each

| Trap | Where it happened | How TransportPipes avoids it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Obligation / chores** ("I have to log in for the clan") | FoE guild battles, Clan Capital weekends, Hay Day Derby | Favours and contributions **bank up** for a few days; weekly goals, no daily streaks that reset; every group activity opt-in; idle production means missing a day never loses ground. |
| **Guilt of letting friends down** | Habitica party damage, Stardew sleep sync, Clash forced wars | Absence never harms others: caretakers keep trades running, the NPC fills unfilled orders and contracts, goals scale to active members, contributions are rewarded per person. Show totals and thanks, **never a list of who didn't contribute**. |
| **Being stalled by an idle partner** | Satisfactory host, Eco on small servers | Every dependency has an automatic fallback (tenant/hub at a markup); nothing waits for anyone to be online. |
| **Feeling exploited / leeching** | Egg Inc. sleepers, FoE snipers, Eve scams | Rewards follow your own contribution (capped), so sleepers take nothing from you; trades stay inside a price band around the hub price; crew trade moves goods, not gold; caps on transfers to young accounts (anti-multi-account). |
| **Empty-world syndrome** | late Travian rounds, Eco low-pop, Anno Online | Tenants in every free seat, pack-up instead of ruins, station merges, lit windows and moving goods, the chronicle and server feed, public trunk lines, server-wide Wonders. Never show a grey, dead estate. |
| **Veteran dominance** | Travian farming, FoE plunder, UO land rush, Eve null-sec, Pokémon Go gyms | No destructive interaction at all; land never scarce (new stations on demand, frontier districts); leagues by stage; held titles decay; veterans' power expresses itself as *help* (donations, trunk lines, mentoring) that is rewarded. |
| **Return shame** | Animal Crossing weeds, villagers leaving | A warm "while you were away" story, nothing broken, the estate kept as a deed, a welcome-back crate, the crew notified ("Tom is back!"). |
| **Leader drama / kicking** | Hay Day neighbourhoods, Egg Inc. co-ops, clans | Stations have no owner who can evict; a crew is small and anyone can leave and move freely; no reason to kick because a quiet member costs nothing. |
| **Forced communication / social anxiety** | public chat MMOs | Positive-only interactions that need no words (favours, thanks, gifts), preset messages; chat optional. |
| **Comparison and envy** | global leaderboards | Local leagues, "your best week" personal records, friend comparisons opt-in. |
| **Friend-gating** | FarmVille neighbour walls | Nothing in progression needs a number of friends; invites reward with cosmetics only. |

## E. What I'd build first (if one developer has days)

1. **Stations + invite link + reserved seats + the station board** (ideas 1–3), with tenants in empty seats. This
   alone answers "three friends join" and "choose a position" and replaces the district mega-network with bounded,
   visible junctions.
2. **Crews and favours** (ideas 4–5): small, cheap, high social value.
3. **Caretakers and deeds, moving house** (ideas 6–7): reuses blueprints; also the migration path for the testers'
   world (every existing estate becomes a deed placed into a station, crews formed from existing neighbours who
   traded most with each other).
4. **Order board** (idea 8), then **station works and trunk lines** (idea 9) for the late game, then leagues and the
   chronicle.

The one rule I would keep above all: **playing together makes you better and never makes you stuck, guilty or prey.**
