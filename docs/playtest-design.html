The AI Playtest

Five AI players, each with a person to play, play the real game in real browsers on a playtest server whose clock we can move forward. Between sessions the world runs on for hours or days, like it does for people. Each player keeps a diary of where it got stuck, what pleased it, and whether it would come back tomorrow. At the end an analyst checks the story against what the design promises.

Real browsersThe players see what a person sees: screenshots and the text on the screen. They act with real clicks, taps and drags. No cheats, no shortcuts into the game.
A clock we can moveA friend group's first five weeks in an afternoon. The world catches up between sessions just as it does after a restart.
Honest diariesEvery claim points to a screenshot or an action number. A player that is stuck says so and gives up, as a person would.

1 · What it should tell us, and what it can't

The balance runs check the economy with one rule-following bot. The e2e scripts check that what I built works the way I expect. Neither finds what I didn't expect: a button nobody sees, a number that contradicts another, a step with no visible way on, a friend who has nothing to do on day 10. That is the playtest's job.

What it can tell us
  • Whether a newcomer finds the way using only what the screen shows (no design docs, no code)
  • Where it gets stuck, and what it expected instead
  • Numbers and texts that contradict each other
  • Looks: overlaps, clipped text, the phone layout
  • Bugs, with exact repro steps from the action log
  • Whether friends have something to talk about and decide together ("who builds glass?")
  • How five weeks go for several players together: who earns what, and whether helping pays
  • Whether each promise of the design happens, and when
What it can't
  • Fun the way people feel it. AI players are patient, read every word and tend to be kind.
  • Waiting: the clock jumps, so nobody feels the eight hours. We judge only what coming back looks like.
  • Fine aiming on the map. I give the players a list of what is where, with tile numbers, to aim by. That makes placing buildings easier for them than for people, so findings about placing count only half.
  • Replace real friends. Its findings are things to fix or to watch for, not a verdict.

2 · The setup at a glance

meThe directorRuns the calendar: moves the clock, starts each session's players (together when they play together), collects the diaries.
serverThe playtest serverThe real server and a fresh world on my machine, with a clock we can move. Not the public test server, so testers never see the AI players.
node + PlaywrightThe driverOne browser per player (desktop or phone). Commands like look, click, tile and drag, all logged with screenshots.
Claude agentsThe playersOne agent per player per session, with its person, its own memory of earlier sessions, and the friends' group chat.
Claude agentThe analystReads all the diaries, logs and numbers, checks the design's promises and writes the report.

director → moves the clock on the server → players act through the driver → diaries and memory → analyst → report → I check every finding with an e2e script and fix it → the same story again

The clock

The server takes all its time from one clock (TimeProvider), and the world catches up to "now" on every tick. The playtest mode swaps in a clock that can jump forward and can run slower during sessions, so that one agent action costs about as much game time as a person's action. Holds, stand-ins, Discoveries, leagues and weekly resets all simply follow.

Snapshots

A copy of the database before each key session, so we can replay day 8 after a fix, or branch the story ("what if Ben never comes back"), without replaying the five weeks.

The friends' group chat Real friends talk outside the game too. Ana, Ben, Cleo and Dev share a group chat (a file the driver shows them), besides the in-game chat. Only the players write in it. What they talk about tells us whether the game gives them something to talk about.

5 · One session, step by step

What a player gets

  1. Its card (who it is, how it plays, its attention budget)
  2. Its memory: what it wrote at the end of its last session, and nothing else
  3. The situation: "Tuesday, 21:00. You have about 20 minutes. Ana wrote in the group: …"
  4. The driver's commands and the rules (below)

The rules

  • Only what the screen shows: no design docs, no code, no cheats
  • An attention budget: Ben 8 actions, Ana 40, Cleo up to 80
  • Stuck protocol: after three tries at the same thing, write STUCK with what you expected, then do something else or stop, as a person would
  • Think aloud only at moments that matter: note "…"
  • End with the diary and the memory update

The driver's commands

CommandWhat it does
lookWhat's on screen as text (top bar, Loady, the open panel, toasts, buttons). With --shot a screenshot too.
mapWhat is around the camera, with tile numbers: your buildings, neighbours', deposits, free land. For aiming.
click "…"A button, tab or link by its visible label
tile x yClicks (or taps) the map on that tile, with the real mouse
drag x1 y1 x2 y2A pipe or an area, really dragged
type, key, cameraTyping, keys, pan/zoom/home
chat, groupThe in-game chat; the friends' group chat
note, endThink aloud into the diary; finish the session

6 · Keeping it honest

The riskWhat we do about it
AI players are kind and say "it's great"Every diary names its three worst moments. Every claim points to an action number or a screenshot. The analyst throws out claims without evidence.
AI players are patient and read everythingAttention budgets, the stuck protocol, and Ben's skimming (the driver shows him only first lines)
Aiming on the map by tile numbers is easier than by eyeFindings about placing count only half. Looks are judged from screenshots only. Each finding is tagged with whether it came from the map list or the screen.
AI players know games and this kind of gameThe cards say "you have never played this game". The memory holds only what the player itself wrote.
Agents think slower than people actThe clock runs slower during sessions, measured in the pilot, so that an action costs about as much game time as a person's
One run is one storyAfter fixing, the same story again from the same snapshots, to compare. Later, a second set of people.
The players see the game's insidesThe driver reads the page only for look and map; acting is real clicks, taps and drags. Cheats are off.

7 · What comes out

The report (a page for you, via dam-tools)

  • The promises: each row of the calendar kept late missed, with the evidence
  • Where people get stuck, ranked by how bad it is and how many players hit it, with screenshots
  • Bugs, each with its repro steps from the action log, plus console and server errors
  • Five weeks of numbers: each player's level and income per day, and the shares from the hub, the Yard, links, Asks and the project. Mia against the crew.
  • Together: what they decided together, what they talked about in the group chat, who helped whom
  • In their words: quotes from the diaries
  • What to change, in order

What I do with it

  1. Reproduce each finding myself with an e2e script, as for any bug (CLAUDE.md)
  2. Fix what's real. Note in the status doc what was a quirk of the AI.
  3. Replay the sessions it touched from their snapshots, to see that it's better
  4. Give you a short list: what was found, what's fixed, and what needs your call
Kept in the repo playtest/ holds the driver, the cards, the stories and the report template. Each run's logs and screenshots stay on my machine (gitignored); only the report goes to you.

8 · Phases and cost

Pilot first: one player, two sessions, to measure what a session costs and to fix the driver where the player can't see or aim. Then the rest with real numbers.

2 · The first eveningAna, Ben and Cleo together, then days 2 and 3~915–25 M tokens
3 · The whole story§17a to day 36 and Mia's track~2550–80 M tokens
4 · After the fixesThe sessions the fixes touched, from their snapshots5–25up to the same

These are guesses: a 20-minute session of about 40 actions, some with screenshots, re-reads its growing context on every step, about 1–3 million input tokens. Most of that is cached re-reading, which is far cheaper than new text. The pilot measures the real number. To keep it down: text by default, screenshots only when asked for and scaled down, and Haiku or Sonnet for the players with Opus only for the analyst.

Who runs the players Claude agents started from my session, on your Claude account. Not the Codex accounts: those are allowed for the art only. The full story is more agents than one workflow should run at once, so I run it phase by phase.

9 · What I would build

  1. Playtest mode for the server. A clock that can jump and can run slower, and admin-only /playtest/advance, /playtest/rate and /playtest/snapshot/restore calls behind a token. Also a dump of events and numbers per company. On only with TransportPipes:Playtest, with cheats off for players, and with its own database.
  2. The driver (playtest/driver.mjs). It keeps a browser open per player across commands (phone or desktop size), and the commands above. Every action is logged with before and after screenshots and with console and server errors. It builds on e2e/lib.mjs (sign-up, guests, codes) and on the engine's tileToScreen for real clicks on tiles.
  3. The cards and the story (playtest/players/*.md, playtest/stories/17a.yaml): who plays when and for how long, what each session starts with, and the promise to check.
  4. The director. It moves the clock, starts the session's players (in parallel when they play together), collects diaries and memories, takes snapshots, and runs the autopilot (the balance bot on the server, in playtest mode only).
  5. The analyst prompt and the report page.

Before any AI plays, I check the tools with a scripted run that drives a player through the driver's own commands.

10 · Questions for you

  1. Pilot first? Two sessions to learn the real cost and how well an AI copes with the map. Then you decide on the first evening and the whole story.
  2. Do you want to play along? You could be a real fifth friend on the first evening. The clock runs normally during sessions, and you'd get a seat code. It would show us at once how the AI players differ from a person.
  3. Anyone to model the players on? Your testers, or how you play yourself. A real person behind a card makes it more truthful than my five.
